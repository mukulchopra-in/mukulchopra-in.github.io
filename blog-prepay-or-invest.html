Prepay the home loan or invest the money? A simple way to compare

A bonus arrives and the question comes up: pay down the home loan, or invest? Here is a straightforward way to compare the two, with a worked example.

Mukul Chopra · 25 October 2026 · 4 min read

It's one of the most common questions I'm asked about home loans. You have some surplus money (a bonus, a maturing FD, an inheritance) and a home loan running at 8–9%. Should you prepay, or invest the money and let the loan run?

There is no single right answer, but there is a simple way to think about it.

Step 1: prepaying is a guaranteed return

Every rupee you prepay stops being charged interest. So prepaying a loan at 8.5% earns you a return of 8.5% a year, guaranteed, with no tax on it, because there is no income, only interest you no longer pay.

That makes the question simple: can you reliably earn more than your loan rate, after tax, somewhere else?

Step 2: adjust for tax

Tax can lower the true cost of your loan, but less often than people think.

  • New tax regime: no deduction for home loan interest on a self-occupied house. Your effective loan rate is the full rate.
  • Old tax regime: interest on a self-occupied house is deductible up to ₹2 lakh a year. If your yearly interest is below ₹2 lakh, part of every rupee of interest is saved through tax, so your effective rate is lower: about 8.5% × (1 − 30%) ≈ 6% in the 30% slab. If your interest is above ₹2 lakh, as it is in the early years of most loans of ₹25 lakh or more, the extra interest gets no deduction. Prepaying then saves interest at the full 8.5%.

Step 3: compare with what the investment earns after tax

  • Fixed deposits and debt: a 7% FD taxed at 30% gives you about 4.9%. That is well below an 8.5% loan. Prepaying wins clearly.
  • Equity mutual funds: they can earn more over long periods, but the return is neither guaranteed nor steady. Long-term gains above ₹1.25 lakh a year are taxed at 12.5%.

So the real comparison is between a guaranteed 8.5% and an uncertain return from equities.

A worked example

Say you have ₹40 lakh outstanding at 8.5% with 15 years left. Your EMI is ₹39,390. You receive ₹5 lakh.

Option A: prepay ₹5 lakh and keep the EMI the same. You save about ₹10.5 lakh of interest and the loan ends 39 months (3 years 3 months) early. For those last 39 months, you invest the ₹39,390 you no longer pay as EMI.

Option B: invest ₹5 lakh in an equity fund for the full 15 years and let the loan run as it is.

Here is what each option is worth at the end of the 15 years, after long-term capital gains tax, for different returns:

The break-even is about 9.4% a year before tax, earned every year for 15 years. Below that, prepaying wins. Above it, investing wins, and by more the higher the return goes. But option B's return is not guaranteed, while option A's is.

You can try your own numbers with the prepayment calculator.

Step 4: what the numbers don't show

  • Emergency fund first. Keep at least six months of expenses, including the EMI, in an easily accessible place before you prepay. Money paid into a loan is hard to get back; a top-up loan takes time and costs interest.
  • Costlier debt first. Clear credit card dues, personal loans and car loans before the home loan. They cost far more.
  • Your job and income. If your income is uncertain, cash in hand is worth more than a slightly smaller loan.
  • How much risk you can live with. Equities can fall 30% in a bad year. If that would worry you, the guaranteed return of prepaying is worth something in itself.
  • Peace of mind. Many people simply sleep better with a smaller loan. That counts.

A practical approach

  1. Build or top up your emergency fund.
  2. Clear any costlier debt.
  3. If you are a cautious investor, or your loan rate is above about 9%, lean towards prepaying.
  4. If you are investing for 10 years or more, are comfortable with equity ups and downs, and already invest regularly, lean towards investing.
  5. If you can't decide, split the money. Half and half captures much of the benefit of both.
When you prepay, keep the EMI and reduce the tenure. That saves far more interest than keeping the tenure and reducing the EMI. Lenders cannot charge prepayment penalties on floating-rate home loans taken by individuals, but fixed-rate loans may carry a charge, so check your sanction letter first.

This is general information, not personal financial advice. Tax rules are as at the date of this post.
