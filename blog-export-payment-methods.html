LC, documentary collection or advance payment: which one fits your export deal?

The payment method decides who carries the risk in an export deal. Here is how the five main options compare, and five questions that point you to the right one.

Mukul Chopra · 25 October 2026 · 5 min read

Exporters spend a lot of time negotiating price, and often far less on payment terms. Yet the payment method decides who carries the risk of something going wrong: the buyer not paying, the goods being stuck at a foreign port, or money being held up by a bank or a country. A good price on bad terms can be a poor deal.

There are five main options. From the exporter's point of view, they run from safest to riskiest.

1. Advance payment

The buyer pays before you ship, in full or in part.

  • Risk to you: almost none. The buyer carries all of it.
  • Cost: just the cost of an inward remittance.
  • Fits when: the order is small, the goods are made to order, the buyer is new and you have the stronger bargaining position, or the buyer's country is high-risk.

Buyers rarely like paying everything upfront. A common middle path is a part advance, say 20–30%, with the balance under one of the methods below. The advance covers your costs if the buyer walks away.

2. Letter of credit (LC)

The buyer's bank undertakes to pay you if you present documents that comply with the credit's terms.

  • Risk to you: shifts from the buyer to the issuing bank, provided your documents comply. If the issuing bank or its country is a concern, ask for the credit to be confirmed by a bank you trust; the confirming bank then adds its own undertaking.
  • Cost: the buyer pays to open the LC. You usually pay advising, negotiation and any confirmation charges, and a discrepancy fee if your documents are refused.
  • Fits when: the buyer is new, the value is large, the country is unfamiliar, or the buyer needs credit (a usance LC lets the buyer pay later while you still have a bank's undertaking).

The catch is that the protection is only as good as your documents. A discrepant presentation leaves you depending on the buyer's goodwill again. The five most common discrepancies are worth knowing before you choose this route.

3. Documentary collection: documents against payment (D/P)

You ship, then hand the documents to your bank, which sends them to a bank in the buyer's country with instructions to release them only when the buyer pays. The rules are URC 522.

  • Risk to you: the banks don't promise to pay. They only control the documents. If the buyer refuses to pay, the goods are at a foreign port in your name, running up demurrage. You can sell them to someone else at a discount, or ship them back.
  • Cost: much cheaper than an LC, and far less paperwork.
  • Fits when: you know the buyer, the goods are easy to sell to someone else, and the buyer's country has no payment restrictions.
Watch out with air and courier shipments. A sea bill of lading made out "to order" gives control of the goods. An air waybill does not: it names a consignee who can collect the goods without the documents. If the air waybill names the buyer, the buyer may get the goods without paying. Don't consign goods to a collecting bank without its prior agreement either (URC 522 Art. 10).

4. Documentary collection: documents against acceptance (D/A)

As with D/P, but the buyer gets the documents by accepting a bill of exchange payable on a future date, for example 90 days after sight.

  • Risk to you: high. The buyer has the goods and you have only a promise to pay. If the bill is dishonoured at maturity, you have a legal claim but no goods.
  • Cost: low.
  • Fits when: the buyer is long-standing and trustworthy, and needs time to sell the goods before paying. Asking for the bill to be avalised (guaranteed by the buyer's bank) makes it much safer.

5. Open account

You ship, send the documents directly to the buyer, and wait for payment on the agreed date.

  • Risk to you: the highest. You rely entirely on the buyer.
  • Cost: the lowest.
  • Fits when: you have a long, good history with the buyer, or the buyer is a large company that will buy only on open account. Export credit insurance (for example from ECGC) or export factoring can cover much of the risk.

Five questions that point you to the answer

  1. How well do you know the buyer? A first order points towards advance payment or an LC. Several years of on-time payment can justify a collection or open account.
  2. Where is the buyer? Political risk, foreign exchange shortages or payment delays in the buyer's country point towards a confirmed LC, whatever the buyer's own record.
  3. Can the goods be sold to someone else? Standard commodities can be resold if a D/P buyer refuses. Goods made to the buyer's specification often can't, so you need more security.
  4. Does the buyer need credit? If yes, the choice is usually between a usance LC, an avalised D/A bill, or open account backed by insurance.
  5. What will the market bear? If competitors offer open account, insisting on an LC may lose you the order. Price the risk in, or insure it, rather than refusing outright.

Two points for Indian exporters

  • Whatever the method, export proceeds must come in through your bank within the period the RBI allows. Your bank tracks each shipping bill until it is paid, so terms that stretch payment too far create work later.
  • An LC or a confirmed order also makes pre-shipment finance (packing credit) easier to get from your bank, which matters if you need working capital to fulfil the order.
