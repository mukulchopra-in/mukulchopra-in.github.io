The 5 discrepancies I see most often, and how exporters can avoid them

Most refusals under a letter of credit come from the same handful of mistakes. Here they are, why they happen, and what to check before your documents leave your hands.

Mukul Chopra · 25 October 2026 · 7 min read

A letter of credit protects an exporter only as long as the documents comply. The moment a bank finds a discrepancy, its promise to pay falls away. Payment now depends on the buyer agreeing to waive it, which in practice turns your LC into something much closer to a documentary collection. You also lose time, and the issuing bank will usually deduct a discrepancy fee from what it pays you.

The frustrating part is that most discrepancies are avoidable. The same few come up again and again at the trade finance desk. Here are the five I see most often.

1. Late presentation, or presenting after expiry

Two deadlines run at once. Documents must be presented within the presentation period, which is 21 calendar days after shipment if the credit doesn't set one (Art. (c)), and never later than the expiry date in field 31D (Art. (d)). Whichever comes first is your real deadline.

Why it happens: the bill of lading reaches the exporter late, a certificate of origin or inspection certificate takes longer than expected, or the courier to a bank abroad eats up the last few days.

How to avoid it:

  • Work out the last day for presentation on the day you ship, not when the documents are ready. The LC date calculator does this for you.
  • If the credit expires at the issuing bank's counters abroad, your documents must physically reach that bank by the expiry date. Allow for courier time and holidays.
  • When you negotiate the LC, ask for a realistic presentation period in field 48 and an expiry place in your own country.
  • Remember that the latest shipment date is never extended because of a holiday (Art. (c)), even though the expiry date and the last day for presentation are (Art. (a)).

2. Data that conflicts between documents

Documents don't need to be identical to each other, but the data in them must not conflict (Art. (d)). A gross weight of 12,400 kg on the packing list and 12,040 kg on the bill of lading is a conflict. So are different numbers of packages, different port names, or shipping marks that don't match.

Why it happens: different people prepare different documents. The exporter types the invoice and packing list, the forwarder issues the bill of lading, the chamber of commerce issues the certificate of origin and the insurer issues the policy, often each working from a different draft.

How to avoid it:

  • Keep one master data sheet for each shipment: LC number, goods description, quantity, weights, number of packages, marks, ports, vessel and invoice number. Send the same sheet to your forwarder and your insurer.
  • Before presenting, put the key figures from every document side by side and read across. It takes ten minutes and catches most problems.
  • Addresses are treated more leniently: they need not match the credit exactly as long as they are in the same country (Art. (j)). The exception is an applicant's address shown as consignee or notify party on a transport document, which must be exactly as the credit states.

3. Bill of lading problems

The bill of lading is the document that gives control over the goods, so banks look at it closely (Art. ). The common faults are:

  • No dated on-board notation when the bill of lading is a "received for shipment" form or names an "intended vessel". The shipment date then comes from the on-board notation, and without one the document doesn't show shipment at all.
  • Consignee or notify party not as the LC requires, for example "to order" when the credit asks for "to the order of the issuing bank".
  • Not a full set of originals when the LC calls for one.
  • Clauses that make it "unclean", such as "packaging torn" or "cartons wet" (Art. ).
  • Port of loading or discharge different from field 44E or 44F.

How to avoid it: send the exact bill of lading wording from field 46A to your forwarder before shipment, and ask to see the draft bill of lading before it is issued. Correcting a draft is easy; correcting an issued original usually means surrendering the full set and getting a new one.

4. Commercial invoice problems

The invoice is the document most often checked against the credit word by word. Under Art. , it must appear to be issued by the beneficiary, be made out in the name of the applicant, and be in the same currency as the credit. The usual faults are:

  • Goods description that doesn't correspond with field 45A. It need not be a mirror image (Art. (c)), but it must not leave out details the credit specifies or describe the goods differently. Using the credit's own wording is the safest course.
  • Amount over the credit value. A bank may refuse an invoice for more than the credit allows (Art. (b)). Check the amount against field 32B and any tolerance in 39A (Art. ).
  • Missing details the LC asks for, such as the Incoterm (for example "CIF Hamburg Incoterms 2020"), the LC number, the HS code or a proforma invoice reference.
  • Arithmetic errors: unit price multiplied by quantity that doesn't equal the line total.

How to avoid it: build your invoice template from fields 45A and 46A of each new credit, not from your last shipment's invoice.

5. Insurance document problems

Where the exporter arranges insurance (under CIF or CIP), the insurance document is a frequent source of refusals (Art. ):

  • Dated after the shipment date, unless it shows that cover started no later than shipment (Art. (e)).
  • Insured for too little. If the credit doesn't say how much, the minimum is 110% of the CIF or CIP value (Art. (f)).
  • Different currency from the credit.
  • Risks not as the credit requires, for example no war and strikes cover when the credit asks for it (Art. (g)).
  • A cover note instead of a policy or certificate. Cover notes are not accepted (Art. (c)).

How to avoid it: arrange cover before the goods are loaded, give your insurer the credit's insurance clause word for word, and check the 110% arithmetic yourself.

Also worth checking: a draft drawn on the wrong party (it must be drawn on the bank named in field 42A, never on the applicant, Art. (c)), amounts in words and figures that disagree on the draft, and certificates issued by someone other than the party the credit names.

A ten-minute check before you present

  1. Is today within both the presentation period and the expiry date, with time to spare for your bank to check?
  2. Does every document listed in 46A appear, in the number of originals and copies asked for?
  3. Do weights, quantities, packages, marks and ports agree across all documents?
  4. Does the invoice show the beneficiary, the applicant, the credit currency, the 45A description and an amount within the credit?
  5. Does the bill of lading have a dated on-board notation, the right consignee and notify party, and no adverse clauses?
  6. Is the insurance dated no later than shipment, for at least 110%, in the credit currency, against the right risks?
  7. Have you met every condition in 47A?

If a discrepancy is found anyway

All is not lost. A bank that refuses must send a single notice listing every discrepancy, by the end of the fifth banking day after presentation (Art. ). If there is still time before expiry, you can correct the documents and present again. Otherwise, you can ask the buyer to waive the discrepancies. Most buyers who want the goods will, but that is now their choice, not your right.
